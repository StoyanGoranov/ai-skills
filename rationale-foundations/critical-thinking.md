# Critical Thinking as a Practice

## Inquiry

> I want to learn how to think critically. Do not use the learn-by-doing skill because it is not a concept to be understood but a practice to be exercised. Let's explore what actions are involved in critical thinking. Use authoritative sources, if there are any.

## Definition

Critical thinking is best understood as a repertoire of actions, not as “being skeptical” or knowing a list of fallacies. A useful working definition is:

> Careful, reflective thinking directed toward deciding what to believe or do.

There is no single canonical theory. However, three respected sources substantially converge: the American Philosophical Association's [46-expert Delphi study](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf), [Robert Ennis's framework](https://education.illinois.edu/docs/default-source/faculty-documents/robert-ennis/ennisstreamlinedconception_002.pdf), and the [*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* synthesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/critical-thinking/). The Delphi study identifies six broad capacities: interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation, and self-regulation.

## Actions involved in critical thinking

These are better treated as moves that can be revisited than as a fixed sequence.

| Action | What it involves |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Notice a reason to inquire** | Recognize uncertainty, disagreement, an unexplained observation, or a decision whose consequences matter. |
| **2. Suspend premature judgment** | Resist immediately accepting or rejecting the first explanation. “I don't yet know” is a legitimate conclusion. |
| **3. Clarify the question** | State exactly what must be believed, explained, predicted, or decided. Separate it from nearby but different questions. |
| **4. Interpret accurately** | Paraphrase claims charitably; define important terms; resolve ambiguity; understand numbers, diagrams, context, and intended meaning. |
| **5. Expose the reasoning** | Identify the conclusion, supporting reasons, intermediate steps, unstated assumptions, and material that is merely rhetorical or irrelevant. |
| **6. Establish appropriate standards** | Ask what would count as adequate evidence in the situation. A casual preference, engineering decision, criminal verdict, and medical diagnosis demand different standards. |
| **7. Investigate the basis** | Examine observations, data, testimony, methods, and prior knowledge. Judge source competence, possible conflicts, corroboration, relevance, and evidential quality. |
| **8. Generate alternatives** | Form rival explanations, interpretations, courses of action, and counterexamples. Ask what evidence each alternative predicts. |
| **9. Draw and test inferences** | Determine what follows deductively, what is merely probable, whether a causal explanation fits, and whether the conclusion goes beyond the evidence. |
| **10. Make a calibrated judgment** | Accept, reject, act, or suspend judgment. State confidence proportionately rather than treating every conclusion as certain or worthless. |
| **11. Explain the reasoning** | State the result, evidence, assumptions, standards, and inferential steps clearly enough that another person can examine them. Address the strongest serious objection. |
| **12. Audit and correct yourself** | Look for neglected evidence, motivated reasoning, emotional pressure, stereotypes, self-interest, methodological mistakes, and failures to consider alternatives. Revise when warranted. |

[Ennis](https://education.illinois.edu/docs/default-source/faculty-documents/robert-ennis/ennisstreamlinedconception_002.pdf) organizes much of this as clarification, establishing the basis for a judgment, inference, considering alternatives, and integrating these actions into a defensible decision. He explicitly includes identifying assumptions, judging sources and observations, deduction, induction, value judgment, and reasoning temporarily from a position with which one disagrees.

## Dispositions that activate the actions

Knowing how to evaluate evidence is useless if one does not do it when a cherished belief is threatened. Critical thinking therefore also involves habits such as:

- Willingness to inquire
- Desire to be well informed
- Serious consideration of opposing views
- Seeking reasons rather than merely demanding them from others
- Using only as much precision as the situation warrants
- Withholding judgment when evidence is insufficient
- Taking—and changing—a position when reasons become sufficient
- Persisting through confusion without forcing an early answer

Open-mindedness does **not** mean treating every claim equally. It means giving alternatives a fair examination before assigning them unequal credibility.

## Three important corrections

First, critical thinking is not synonymous with criticism. Finding weaknesses is only one part; constructing a better explanation or decision is equally important.

Second, source checking is insufficient. A reliable source can reason badly, while an unreliable source can occasionally state something true. Both the premises and the inferential connection must be evaluated.

Third, critical thinking is not completely domain-independent. One cannot competently judge a clinical study, historical claim, or software-security argument without relevant knowledge of its methods and context. The [Delphi report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED315423.pdf) explicitly warns against reducing critical thinking to generic logical operations, and the [*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* synthesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/critical-thinking/) makes the same point.

## Compact practice protocol

For any significant claim or decision, write answers to these eight prompts:

1. What exactly is the question?
2. What is my current answer, and how confident am I?
3. What are the strongest reasons and evidence?
4. What assumptions connect that evidence to the conclusion?
5. What is the strongest plausible alternative?
6. What evidence would distinguish the alternatives?
7. What would change my mind?
8. After checking, what should I believe or do—and with what confidence?

The written form matters: explanation makes hidden gaps visible, while the final confidence estimate prevents a plausible conclusion from quietly becoming “certain.”

The best place to begin practicing is clarification and argument reconstruction. If the claim, reasons, or assumptions are misidentified, every later evaluation is aimed at the wrong target.
